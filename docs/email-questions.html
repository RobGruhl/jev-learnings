Case study Part 2 of 4 · asking the right questions

Asking the right questions

A decision model is only as good as the question it's asked. On this inbox the same concept scored anywhere from near chance to near perfect depending on wording. This page shows what Jev could judge on real mail, the phrasing experiments that showed how to ask, and the two things no wording fixes.

Seven lessons about wording

Jev

1. Name the near misses

A Choice whose options spell out what the concept is not (an event isn't a deadline, a requested cancellation isn't a failed payment) was the best single question on every concept, and it held on the easy-to-miss positives (0.96–0.99). Describe "none" well: on the money set, a thin "none" option was right only 50% of the time.

Jev

2. Exact propositions are best calibrated

The exact payment Noul reached 88% precision at 0.5. Where a threshold must mean something on its own, use the narrowest true-or-false statement of the concept.

Jev

3. Abstract wording ranks but over-fires

"Involves a deadline" ranked well (0.955) but averaged 0.31 on mail with no deadline, for 29% precision at 0.5. It fails outright when abstraction changes the concept: "personal" isn't "someone is waiting" (0.744).

Jev

4. Framing is concept-specific

"May not realize this is coming" won for hidden charges (0.96, where a flat "is this about money" got 0.65). The same surprise framing was the weakest rung for deadlines and payment failures. It helps only where the trap is not knowing something is coming.

Jev Code

5. Don't decompose what the model already grasps

Splitting a concept into surface facts and learning to combine them lost 0.03–0.08 on deadlines and replies, and tied on payment. The atoms were weak alone ("names a date" 0.59) because the model understands these concepts whole. Decomposition fits concepts that really are conjunctions of checkable facts, like spam signals.

Jev

6. Grade a question against its own concept

"A charge is coming" looked like chance when graded against "needs action" (about 0.5), and was nearly dropped. Graded against the concept it actually asks about, the same idea scored 0.89. Build decisions from concept answers; don't judge a question by a target it wasn't asking about.

Jev Code

7. Batching is free

Every question goes in one request, with the message sent once as shared state. A whole question set over three months of mail cost cents.

Reading the argmax

A Choice gives a distribution and a winner. The distribution ranked well everywhere; the winner was less even. Payment problems were classed right 90% of the time and waiting-on-reply 87%. The deadline Choice was right only 43% overall, because "none" and "informational date" blurred together, but when the answer was "action deadline" it was right 94% of the time. Read the probability of the classes you care about, not just the top class. (Fine-tuned open models later reached 60–66% on the same deadline argmax.)

Two things no wording fixes

Code

It ranks; it doesn't measure

Asked to size things on a Score, Jev ordered them loosely: rank correlation 0.28 against the real money at stake, and 0.15 against time to a deadline. It read a yearly charge of about $120 as "$10–100". Extract the parts (the amount, the date) and do the arithmetic in code.

Human Code

It can't know what isn't in the message

Who the owner knows, what is already open, what they've ruled before. The worst filed items in the first real session were all of this kind (part 3). That knowledge belongs in rules and records, which are cheap and exact.

Hosted against local

The hosted model was stronger on judgment-heavy questions: routine-ness as a ranker for action mail (0.911 against 0.865), same-matter matching (0.980 against 0.955) and resolution (0.933 against 0.873). Some failures were shared: an early "charge coming" question was at chance on both, so the phrasing was at fault, not the model's size. Phrasing also didn't transfer cleanly to other models: on a 2B open decider, consequence and surprise framings for waiting-on-reply fell 0.20 AUC. Re-grade a question set for every model you move it to.
