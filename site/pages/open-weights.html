Evidence Measured October 14, 2026

Open weights caught up

In early October the open decision models we tried were a step behind Jev. Two weeks later a wave of 26–27B open models arrived, so we rented a GPU and ran six of them through the same harnesses. Most now match hosted Jev on our private tasks, and the best edge it out. What's left is the cost of hardware and, for some, speed.

The pick: Kev-27B. It is the only open model that matched hosted Jev in the full runs as well as the screen. It ties Jev on the mail sorter (ceiling 63.3% against 63.6%) and on wine with 38 questions (1.83 against 1.86), and it beats Jev asking for the wine score directly (2.13 against 2.31). It also named the exact deadline class most often (66% against 43%), and it answers a mail message in about 0.4 s on one H100.

  • If you need a true 26B model, or the most speed: GEV-26B-Decide is a mixture of experts with about 4B parameters active, at about 0.3 s per message. It is level with Jev within noise, just behind Kev.
  • Highest public benchmark score: pplx-decider v1.1 had the top JevBench score (207 of 231) and d3 the best mail AUCs. Both take 4–5 s per message on their published NVIDIA serving path, so they didn't get the full runs.

Approximate by design. This was a screen, not a full replication: every model got the public benchmark, the page fixtures and the mail concept probes, and only the two fastest got the full mail sorter and wine runs. Single runs, one GPU, BF16 weights. The mail and wine sets are private, so they can't have leaked into training. JevBench is public and might have.

  • Kev ties Jev with 38 questions (−0.03 RMSE, 95% CI −0.07 to +0.01) and beats it asking for the score directly (−0.18, CI −0.25 to −0.11). Its answers track Jev's closely (median per-question correlation 0.93), and adding them to Jev's helps a little (1.82).
  • As a feature source for the sorter, Kev's ceiling is within a third of a point of Jev's. That's the gap that sank the earlier open models: the 2B decider's ceiling was 18 points below.
  • GEV-26B is a hair behind Kev and level with Jev: +0.04 RMSE with 38 questions (CI −0.01 to +0.09) and −0.01 directly, both ties; its sorter ceiling is 0.7 points under Jev's. It is the fastest of the fine-tuned models, and adding its answers to Jev's gives 1.83.

d3, pplx-decider~4–5 sTheir published NVIDIA path is plain transformers behind a one-request lock
Hosted Jev~0.05 s8 requests in flight, someone else's GPUs

These are bench timings through an SSH tunnel, not tuned serving. d3's authors report 27 requests a second through a batching engine on AMD hardware, which we didn't try. The point stands: the serving path matters as much as the model, and you find out only by running it.

What this changes

  1. Quality is no longer the reason to pay for hosted. On our private tasks Kev-27B, d3 and pplx-decider are at or slightly above Jev. Choose on privacy, volume, latency and operations instead.
  2. The fine-tune matters more than the base. OpenJev, the only model here without decision training, trailed every fine-tuned model on mail despite being the fastest.
  3. Exact-class answers improved most. Concept AUCs barely moved, but the best open models named the right deadline class 60–66% of the time against Jev's 43%. If you read the argmax, not just a probability, that's the biggest change.
  4. Count the hardware. Each model wants an 80 GB GPU at full precision. At $3.29 an hour, one GPU-hour buys about 50,000 typical hosted Jev requests (about 1.5k tokens each), so open weights pay off at steady high volume or when data can't leave your hands, not for occasional use.
  5. Re-grade and refit on switch. Phrasing still transfers imperfectly (OpenJev's best reply question was a Score, not the Choice every other model preferred), and thresholds belong to a model, runtime and rubric triple.
