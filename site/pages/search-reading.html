Let the model read

The usual design puts keyword search first, because reading everything sounds too expensive. A decision model changes that. Jev read every paragraph in the corpus for 23 cents, and on the question we traced, that showed where facts had been lost: at the keyword gate, before any model saw them. After that, the main lesson was about depth. Reading further down a list moved results far more than rewording the question.

Don't plan on the burst. The 2 s figure is a rested account. The account's documented limit is 100,000 tokens a second, which would make this read about 56 s. We measured far above that and can't explain why. Budget for the sustained rate, and treat your rate limit as part of the design.

Keyword gates can lose facts

A trace of one weak answer showed the leak. BM25 had reached only 6 to 12% of the podcast paragraphs, because spoken paragraphs often never name their subject. The BM25 bars are floors, so don't compare them with Jev's. Only graded paragraphs could count, and the grading pool came mostly from Jev's lists: 95 to 97% of each BM25 list was never graded. So this chart can't say Jev's top 250 beats BM25's top 5,000. The firmer evidence is the trace above, plus what 5,000 means in practice: at that depth, a keyword list still leaves Jev to read about a fifth of the corpus. Keyword search is free, and it finds exact names that Jev may rank low. We now use it as a second list rather than as a gate, but that choice still needs a blind test on fresh questions.

What each tier ended up doing

Code

Exact and free

Grep for names, date filters, deduplication, blending rankings, tracking what's been read and what it cost. Reliable when it adds to a list, risky when it removes from one.

Jev

Judgment at scale

One statement about each paragraph, then rank by score. Also decides where a passage ends, and rereads with a draft in hand.

LLM

Reasoning and prose

Writes the draft and the answer, and can choose the search. Most of the time goes here: about 10 of the stack's 14 s.

Reading further beats rewording

Jev's top list took the same statement each time:

This paragraph states something an answer to the question in `question` would rely on.

We tested 16 ways of asking, and Opus proposed six more after reading low-ranked useful paragraphs. Opus graded 8,363 paragraphs blind to rank. Rerunning the same wording moves results about 1 point, so that's the noise floor.

"Useful" is the share of all useful paragraphs in the graded pool that the list reached. No wording reliably improved the top 40. Most wordings that gained on tuning questions lost the gain on held-out ones. Only rely plus a graded Score held on both, and only by +4.3 and +2.1 points at top 100. That differs from email, where wording moved accuracy a lot. Our best guess is that a ranking cares less about the wording, and that most misses here come from what Jev is shown. We haven't tested that explanation.

Wording also changes the score scale. On the textbooks, the share of paragraphs scoring 0.5 or more ran from 0.8% to 12.9% across wordings. A fixed "hot" threshold belongs to one wording, so use ranks.

Many wordings at once

We also had Jev score each paragraph with 21 wordings, one pass per wording, and blended the saved scores ten ways, from a simple max to a learned ranker. Blending helped modestly, mostly past the top 40: over all 24 questions, mean of log-odds gained +5.7 points at top 100 and a CatBoost ranker +9.2, each judged on questions it wasn't trained on. CatBoost also used source, length, digit share and headings, and trained on tuning questions and judged on held-out ones, it gained +4.1. Max was the least steady. Part 3 has every wording, every blend and what the extra wordings cost.
