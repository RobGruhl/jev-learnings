1 · Asking Jev whether the evidence is enough

This ran first, on the earlier textbook corpus. The idea was a router: after gathering evidence, ask Jev whether it is enough, then answer, search again or decline. To test it, we removed one intended source from the evidence and checked whether Jev's "enough" score moved.

What we took from it. The signal was too small: a median drop of 0.013 under a spread of 0.123 between questions. Per-part checks did no better as a gate. They pointed at the gap only by comparison with a full copy that production never has. So the loop doesn't ask permission to stop. Later, on the other corpus, one draft-aware reread helped and a second added nothing (episode 5), so it runs one and stops. Part 4 has the details.

2 · A tuning win that didn't hold

Five waves of optimization ran on 16 tuning questions: search depth, hot-list shapes, gap checks, writers and trims. One design stood out by a wide margin.

What we took from it. On 16 questions, the best of 40 or more variants is flattered by selection: about half its gain survived a rerun, and none carried over to new questions. Both the rerun and the held-out check were cheap, and both were needed: the rerun showed part of the gain was real on those questions, and the held-out set showed it didn't carry over. The tuned design stays a candidate until 40 or more fresh questions settle it.

3 · Tracing one weak answer to the gate

The search video in part 1 shows the tuned stack stating 2 of 7 facts on one held-out question. Instead of tuning again, we traced where each missing fact had gone.

What we took from it. The losses weren't all at the gate. On this run, paragraphs dropped somewhere between the keyword search and the hot list, and the untuned run through the same gate kept most of them. But a full read ranked every fact paragraph in the top 134, with no step before it that could drop them. It costs $0.233 a question, about twice the rest of the stack, and about 2 s when rested (7 to 12 s back to back). That's affordable, though not free. This is one question, and the comparison of whole lists across all 24 is weaker than it looks (see part 2). Still, it changed the design more than the five waves did: Jev reads everything, and BM25 and grep add exact hits beside it.

4 · Hunting for a better wording

With every paragraph read, the next question was how to ask. Opus graded the lists, studied what ranked badly and proposed new statements aimed at the patterns it found.

What we took from it. The diagnosis was good, and it pointed away from wording. A table row without its header can't be rescued by rephrasing the question. Reading further down the list moved results far more: 96 gold facts in the top 40, 129 in the top 250. Blends of many wordings helped a little past the top 40. Part 3 has every wording and blend.

5 · A second pass that reads the draft

An open question is hard to judge paragraphs against. A draft answer makes it concrete: Jev can ask whether a paragraph adds to it, corrects it or expands on it. It was the author's idea, and it produced the clearest answer-level gain of the tuning work.

What we took from it. Giving Jev more to read against helped more than asking it differently. The blend with rely mattered too: the combined statement alone found 21 missed facts in 20 added paragraphs, and the blend found 28. Some caveats. Most of the +8.8 may come from adding 20 paragraphs at all: against simply reading 20 further down rely, the edge is +5.3 [−0.2, 11.1], not settled. The blend was chosen after seeing seven methods, on questions we had all inspected. The scored run reread every paragraph for $0.29. Replaying saved scores, the combined statement on rely's top 2,000 found the same facts as on the full corpus, for about $0.02. A third pass added nothing.

6 · Opus runs its own search

Last, we handed Opus the tools and a page of these findings and let it choose. This is one real run, with tool names and paragraph counts only.

What we took from it. Opus followed the playbook's moves, which is expected, since the playbook recommended them: a full read on 20 of 24 questions, grep on all 24 and a draft-aware reread on 23. It wrote shorter answers and reached a different 16 facts. That makes "run both and merge" the obvious next test. It isn't a free win: by estimate the orchestrator cost about twice the pipeline as scored, and about three times a pipeline using the cheap reread. Neither cost was measured end to end. Part 5 has the full comparison.

The pattern across all six

Failed

Small signals and small samples

The sufficiency gate's signal was smaller than the spread between questions. The tuned win and the wording winners were flattered by selection over many tries on 16 questions. Each looked good until it was checked somewhere it hadn't been chosen.

Worked

Giving Jev more to read

All of the paragraphs instead of a keyword shortlist, then a draft to read them against. Code combined the rankings instead of replacing one with another.

Draw

Letting the LLM choose

It followed the playbook's moves and covered different ground. It looks most useful beside the pipeline rather than instead of it.

How far to trust these. These six are real runs with their measured numbers. Only the held-out check in episode 2 and the held-out results in episode 4 used questions set aside for testing. All 24 questions have now been inspected, so episodes 3 to 6 need fresh questions before they count as settled.
