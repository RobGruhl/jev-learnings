Searching a corpus, the testbed

The third project asks a different question from the first two. Instead of judging one message or one game state, can a decision model help answer a hard question that no single document answers? That means finding and reading scattered sources, quickly and cheaply, in material the LLM has never seen. This six-part case study shows what we built, what moved the results, and what fooled us. It uses public documents about one large retailer, and it reports numbers and our own prompts only. No corpus text appears here.

The short answer: yes, but as a loop rather than a one-way funnel. Jev should read everything, code should add exact lookups and blending, and the LLM should write a draft that sends Jev back for one more pass.

The ten findings

  1. The stack is faster than an LLM alone on unseen material, and probably reaches more. First words came sooner on all 8 held-out questions. It stated about 27 facts of 51 against 14, but that margin is uncertain and probably generous.
  2. Jev works best as the reader, not as a filter after keyword search. It read 28,167 paragraphs for $0.233.
  3. Code is risky as a gate and valuable alongside Jev. BM25 reached only 6 to 12% of podcast paragraphs on a traced question, and Jev's full read found what it lost.
  4. How far down you read matters more than how you ask. No wording reliably improved the top 40, while reading to 250 added 33 facts.
  5. A draft answer makes Jev a much better second reader. Stated facts rose from 103 to 116, and a reread of the top 2,000 costs about $0.02. A third pass added nothing.
  6. Jev is good glue for passage boundaries. Turning off Jev-decided growth lost 13.4 points of evidence.
  7. Spend the LLM on judgment and writing. An Opus orchestrator tied the pipeline at 116 of 148, on noticeably different facts. Together they reach 132.
  8. Source structure is now the main constraint. Flattened tables and rough transcripts lose facts that no wording recovers.
  9. Don't rely on an "is this enough?" gate. It barely noticed when a whole source was removed.
  10. Evaluation fooled us repeatedly. A design that won 69 to 52 on tuning questions lost 26 to 27 on held-out ones.

How far to trust these. Only finding 1 and part of the blending result come from questions set aside for testing, and all 24 questions have now been inspected. Treat findings 3 to 7 as directional until they run on 40 or more fresh questions. This corpus also isn't very large: part 5 covers what scale would change.

The corpus

The sources run from 2002 to October 2026: real, messy and in two languages. They hold information outside an LLM's memory, which the earlier test on open textbooks could not promise. Is the answer already in the model's head?

Mostly not. With no evidence, Sonnet stated 0 of 76 required facts dated after its cutoff and 1 of 72 dated before it, though partial memory reaches about 16 of those 72. So a good score here comes from search. Each answer was scored fact by fact and blind, twice, with a third pass on ties. Facts stated counts what the answer says, not what was retrieved.

The stack against an LLM alone

This first stack used a keyword prefilter, a Jev wave, Jev-grown passages and one Sonnet answer. The baseline was the same writer searching for itself with grep and read. Three arms ran live on the 8 held-out questions, twice each:

ArmFacts stated of 51Median totalFirst wordsMedian cost
The stack27.014.3 s5.1 s$0.103
The tuned stack26.014.1 s5.0 s$0.129
LLM alone with grep and read14.021.1 s15.5 s$0.093

The speed result is firm: first words came sooner on all 8 questions, and the full answer finished sooner on 7. Jev is a small part of that time, about 1.5 to 2 s. The gain comes from replacing several model turns with one. The fact lead is less firm: +26.0 points [8.6, 41.2], better on 6 questions and worse on 1. The LLM-alone arm stopped at a median of 5 turns of 12 and was never pushed to search harder, and an earlier closed-book run over all 24 questions showed a lead of about 5 points [−4.6, 14.7]. Its low cost also depends on prompt caching: about $0.17 without it, by estimate.

Five waves of tuning found nothing confirmed

Five rounds of optimization ran on the 16 tuning questions, testing search depth, hot lists, gap checks, writers and trims. One design looked like a big win, and then it wasn't:

The tuned design stayed a candidate and the defaults didn't change. The waves were still useful, because they showed what doesn't matter. The real gains came next, when a trace of one weak answer pointed at the keyword gate.
